## Hypothesis

Ukraine proved that the dominant unit of modern warfare is the small, cheap, attritable autonomous platform — not the $100M fighter jet or the $3B destroyer. The US defense industrial base is structurally misaligned with this reality: it is built around large prime contractors (LMT, RTX, HII) that produce exquisite, expensive, low-volume systems. The market is correctly pricing the legacy primes as durable cash flows, but is significantly underpricing the smaller companies building high-volume, low-cost autonomous systems — because their revenues are small and their procurement wins are irregular. The inflection point is FY2026-2027 defense budgets, when autonomous systems programs move from R&D line items to program-of-record procurement at meaningful volumes. The companies with proven hardware and software integration today will capture disproportionate share of that transition.

## Bull vs. Bear — Steelman Both

**Best bull argument:** The US military is at a genuine doctrinal inflection point. Ukraine proved the old model is obsolete in peer-competitor conflict. China is building autonomous systems at scale. The US has a 2-3 year window to reset procurement before the gap becomes dangerous. Congressional support is bipartisan (rare). The companies building the right systems today are small enough that single program wins are company-making events. You can own the entire Kratos market cap for what the Air Force spends on one F-35.

**Best bear argument:** US defense procurement is the most bureaucratic, slow-moving, captured process in the world. The legacy primes (Lockheed, Raytheon, Boeing) have relationships, congressional seats, and contractual moats that take decades to dislodge. "Autonomous systems will replace legacy platforms" has been the correct strategic prediction and the wrong investment for 15 years. The procurement machinery will absorb the lessons of Ukraine into committee reports and working groups, not purchase orders, by 2027.

**Why the long survives the bear:** The conviction is lower (3/5) precisely because of the bear case. The trade-off: you're not betting on procurement reform broadly — you're betting that *some* program-of-record awards happen for proven systems. The base case doesn't require full doctrine change, just partial. At current small-cap valuations, partial wins are sufficient.

## What Would Change My Mind

- Two NDAA cycles (FY2026 and FY2027) where autonomous systems programs receive flat or declining funding vs. request
- Major autonomous systems program cancellation (e.g., CCA program cancelled or consolidated into legacy prime)
- Evidence that KTOS/AVAV are losing competitive bids to legacy primes who are bringing autonomous systems in-house

## Notes

Lower conviction than the other two theses (3/5 vs 4/5) because the procurement timing risk is genuinely high. This is better sized smaller, or entered after a specific catalyst (CCA down-select, NDAA passage) that confirms the spending direction.

Contrast trade to consider: SHORT legacy primes (HII, LMT) on a multi-year view if autonomous systems genuinely displaces high-unit-cost platforms. Not actionable on a 2027 horizon but worth tracking.

Comparable situation: Shift from mainframe to PC-era defense computing. The correct prediction (distributed computing would win) was made in the 1980s; the procurement actually shifted in the 1990s. You needed to be early but not too early.
